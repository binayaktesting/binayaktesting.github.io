Modeling forest
growth and survival

My foundation in applied analytics: using field measurements to study how loblolly pine stands grow and survive.

The result
The selected dominant height model achieved an average root mean square error of 2.316 feet across two validation folds.

  • SAS

Read the research story +

The research question

Forest planning depends on understanding how stands change over time. My master’s research at Mississippi State University focused on growth and survival in loblolly pine.

The data

I analyzed historical measurements collected from 1981 to 1989 by the Mississippi State and Virginia Tech forestry research cooperatives. The data covered 377 plots in 115 stands, aged 4 to 34 years, across Alabama, Mississippi, Louisiana and Arkansas.

My modeling work

I compared nonlinear equations for dominant height, diameter and survival in SAS and R. The methods included a generalized algebraic difference approach, or GADA, to the Chapman Richards height model, three stage least squares for diameter equations, and logistic regression within a two step mortality model.

How I evaluated the models

I split the data into two sets, fitted models on one set and evaluated predictions on the other, then swapped their roles. I compared the resulting errors and refitted the selected equations using the combined data.

The results

The selected dominant height model had validation errors of 2.214 and 2.417 feet, giving an average root mean square error of 2.316 feet. After refitting on the combined data, the model had a fit index of 0.973 and a root mean square error of 2.294 feet. These final fit statistics describe the refitted model; the separate validation results assess prediction error.

Where the findings apply

The research focused on plantations with limited management inputs. Applying these equations to intensively managed plantations or different regions would require further evaluation. Integration into the CoLob growth and yield simulator was proposed as future work in the thesis.

What I bring from this work

I learned to connect statistical assumptions with the conditions behind the data. Field measurement experience helps me ask how information was collected, where errors might enter, and whether a model’s output makes sense in practice.

Research source

Binayak Bartaula. 2017. Master’s thesis, Mississippi State University. The university’s open access record provides the full title and thesis download; model evaluation results are reported in Tables 4.1 and 4.2.

A practical question about a service families depend on: what factors help explain school bus delays?

Bus company features emerged as leading predictors in the project analysis.

The problem

For my MSDS 692 practicum at Regis University, I explored patterns in reported school bus incidents and modeled their recorded delay durations. The data describe reported incidents, so this analysis does not estimate the chance that any scheduled trip will be late.

My work

I standardized dates and company names, joined daily weather observations, compared patterns across boroughs and operators, and encoded company and borough categories for regression. The notebook records 711,939 rows after the weather join and 654,519 modeling records after borough filtering and removal of 124 rare target values.

Model comparison

I compared 100 tree Random Forest and XGBoost models using a random 80% training and 20% test split. The saved notebook outputs report:

Historical model evaluation
ModelRMSE, minutesR²
Random Forest17.780.26
XGBoost17.680.27

These results show modest predictive performance. RMSE gives greater weight to larger errors; it is not the average absolute error. The saved scores have not yet been independently reproduced from the original raw data.

The takeaway

Company indicators were prominent in XGBoost’s feature importance results. The Selby and Pride indicators had the highest scores, approximately 0.215 and 0.165. These describe the model’s use of the features and do not establish that a company caused a delay.

Limitations and next steps

The original preparation converts missing delay durations to zero and extracts only the first number from each delay description. Those choices require revision before treating the scores as a reliable benchmark. A stronger evaluation would fit preprocessing on training data, compare a simple baseline, and reserve later dates for testing. Future forecasts would also need weather information available at prediction time.

The project includes an analysis notebook, a presentation dated October 16, 2025, charts and a sample dataset. This case study uses the notebook’s recorded scores because the presentation reports different values.

Random Forest exceeded the majority baseline on the saved test set, but performance was uneven across age groups.
  • Python

Read the case study +

The question

For my MSDS 696 practicum, I investigated whether listening patterns and artist metadata could help distinguish age groups. The project connects data collection, record matching, feature engineering and model evaluation.

My work

I selected 10,000 Last.fm users, collected Spotify artist metadata and joined the sources. After cleaning and aggregation, the supplied modeling dataset contains 9,357 users. I combined genre TF-IDF features with listening volume, weighted artist popularity, gender and country information.

What the evaluation shows

The saved test confusion matrices give 69.02% accuracy for Random Forest and 66.93% for Logistic Regression. Always predicting the largest age group would achieve 63.62% on those same 1,872 test users. Separately, the notebook reports a mean accuracy of 68.33% for Random Forest across ten folds.

Why accuracy needs context

Adults aged 21 to 40 make up most of the dataset. The Random Forest classification report has a macro F1 score of 0.32, with very low recall for children and older users. Overall accuracy alone therefore overstates how well the model recognizes every group.

Limits and further work

The original vectorizer was fitted before evaluation splits, so a stronger comparison needs preprocessing fitted within each training fold. The historical user sample and artist matching also limit generalization. These results describe the saved experiment and have not been independently reproduced through the full collection workflow.

What this project demonstrates

Combining data from different sources, transforming text into model features, and examining class imbalance before making claims about predictive performance.

DATA INGESTION · DATABASE DESIGN

Making weather records
available through an API

View the code on GitHub ↗, opens in a new tab

A Django application that ingests station observations and exposes daily records and annual summaries through a searchable data service.

The approach
Defined database models, implemented bulk ingestion and added REST endpoints with station, date and year filters.
The result
A source project with migrations, an ingestion command, API documentation and automated tests for data loading and filtered responses.
  • Python
  • Django
  • REST Framework
  • SQLite
Read the case study +

The problem

Daily observations stored in separate station files are difficult to query consistently. This project organizes them into a database and provides a common interface for retrieving records and summaries.

Implementation

The ingestion service reads tab separated station files and uses bulk database inserts. A station and date uniqueness constraint prevents duplicate daily records. Separate models store daily observations and annual station statistics.

The API

The daily records endpoint supports station and date filters; the statistics endpoint supports station and year filters. Serializers produce JSON responses. The source also includes Swagger documentation and three automated tests covering ingestion, daily records and annual summaries.

Scope and validation

The supplied archive contains 167 station files covering 1985 through 2014. Source review confirms the API structure and test cases. The application has not been independently run in this review, so no deployed service or measured performance improvement is claimed.

Further development

Annual summaries need more precise handling of missing measurements and repeated calculation runs. Tests should cover partial missing records, units and repeated ingestion before deployment.

Removed duplicates, expanded multiple genre labels and used grouped summaries, bar charts and a heatmap to compare patterns.

The questions

For MSDS 670 at Regis University, I compared how frequently genres appear, how their reported budgets and revenue differ, and how those measures vary over time.

My work

The notebook starts with 10,866 records. It removes a duplicate and records without genre labels, expands films into their individual genres, and summarizes the resulting data with Pandas. Matplotlib bar charts compare categories, while a Seaborn heatmap shows annual patterns.

Reading the figures carefully

The saved genre count uses distinct film titles, which can combine different films sharing a name. Multiple genre membership means category counts overlap. These counts describe the supplied catalog; they do not directly measure audience preference.

Financial interpretation

The notebook calculates reported revenue minus production budget. That measure excludes distribution, marketing and other costs, so it is not net profit or return on investment. Missing financial data recorded as zero and changes in price levels also affect comparisons.

Examining U.S. debt

An Excel analysis of historical debt balances, annual changes, monthly summaries and an exploratory forecasting exercise.

Organized historical records, calculated percentage changes, built pivot tables and used Excel’s exponential smoothing forecast function.
The focus
Distinguish total debt outstanding from debt held by the public, and separate historical observations from model projections.
  • Excel

Read the case study +

The question

How have U.S. debt balances changed over time, and what can spreadsheet summaries reveal about their historical pattern?

Workbook structure

The workbook contains five sheets covering raw records, prepared data, annual percentage changes, monthly history and projected growth. It includes two pivot tables, three charts and FORECAST.ETS formulas.

The historical scope

The supplied records cover April 1, 1993 through February 15, 2023. The latest observation is approximately $31.45 trillion in total public debt outstanding. This is a historical dataset, not a current debt report.

Forecast interpretation

The forecast sheet models annual maximum debt held by the public, which is different from total debt outstanding. Later forecast formulas incorporate earlier projected values. The exercise illustrates spreadsheet forecasting; it should not be treated as a current fiscal projection.

Further validation

The prepared data include four repeated dates, and 2023 covers only part of the year. Comparing monthly averages across a rising time series also requires care before claiming seasonality. A stronger forecast would use a fixed training period and compare predictions with later observations.
